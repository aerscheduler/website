Nothing about Airworthiness Directives is on by default. <Where app="web">Settings → Airworthiness
Directives</Where> has four choices: do nothing, track them here yourself, tell us they are tracked
somewhere else, and a fourth that is not built yet. The same page shows what we could actually
match for each of your aeroplanes.
</Summary>

<Roles>Owners, Admins</Roles>

## Why this is a choice and not a feature

Plenty of schools use AerScheduler for oil changes, annuals and the 100-hour, and have no
interest in us being involved in Airworthiness Directives. Plenty of others already pay for
ADlog, AVTRAK or a Tdata subscription and have a mechanic who owns the whole question.

Turning a catalogue on for those schools would not be a helpful default. It would be several
hundred candidate directives nobody asked for, attached to a subject where a flood of alerts
teaches people to stop reading alerts.

So it is off, and it stays off until somebody chooses otherwise.

## The four choices

<Screenshot id="ad-tracking-modes" />

<Steps>
  <Step n={1} title="Not here">
    We do nothing about ADs. Maintenance works exactly as it does now.

    You can still set an inspection's **Where this comes from** to *Airworthiness Directive* if
    you want the badge and the permanent compliance record. That is recordkeeping and it costs
    nothing. Nothing is proposed, nothing is watched, nobody is notified.
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="I track them here">
    You enter the ADs, we keep the records. The AD badge, the permanent compliance record, the
    Compliance log and the Airworthiness compliance report.

    We still do not tell you which ADs apply. You add the ones your IA or your AD subscription
    identifies.
  </Step>
  <Step n={3} title="Watch for new ones (not available yet)">
    **This is not built.** The option is on the page so you can see where we are going, and it
    cannot be selected.

    When it exists, it will read newly published Airworthiness Directives and flag the ones
    naming your make, model or serial number, and somebody at your school will decide for each
    one whether it applies to a given aeroplane. That decision will be recorded against a name
    and a certificate, and it will be permanent.

    Until then, **nothing in AerScheduler watches for new Airworthiness Directives**. Whatever
    you use today to find out that one has been issued, keep using it.
  </Step>
  <Step n={4} title="Somewhere else">
    Applicability is tracked in another system. Name it, and we propose nothing and watch
    nothing.

    The name is printed on any AD document we produce, so a reader knows where the authoritative
    list lives rather than assuming it is ours.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="important">
Whatever you choose, AerScheduler never decides that an Airworthiness Directive does **not**
apply to your aircraft, and today it does not tell you that one exists either. Finding the
directives that apply to your fleet is still yours to do. What this product does is keep the
record of what you complied with, and when.

That determination belongs to a certificated person, and it is the one thing software getting
it wrong could actually hurt somebody over.
</Callout>

## What a serial number buys you

An AD names the aircraft it applies to by make and model, and usually by a serial number range:
*"Model PC-12/47E airplanes, manufacturer serial numbers 2001 through 2999"*. Two 172s parked
next to each other are frequently not both affected.

The same settings page shows, per aeroplane, how precisely we could match:

<Screenshot id="ad-readiness-panel" />

- **By serial.** We can check the range an AD names and tell whether your aeroplane falls inside
  it.
- **By model only.** We can tell that an AD mentions your make and model, but not whether your
  particular aircraft is in the affected range. You will be shown more candidates than actually
  apply, and somebody has to read them.
- **Not enough detail.** We do not know the make or model, so we cannot propose anything.

That panel is a statement of what we could match, not something running. Nothing in
AerScheduler proposes directives today, because proposing them is *Watch for new ones* and that
mode is not built.

What a serial number earns you now is on the paperwork. The **Airworthiness compliance** report
prints **Make and model** and **Serial** beside the tail number. Those two are the whole of how
a directive states applicability, so it can be checked against the aeroplane on the page you hand
an inspector instead of somebody walking out to the hangar to read a data plate.

Recording one need not be data entry. Picking a tail from the lookup on the aircraft form can
fill the serial in along with the make, model and year, so an aeroplane added that way arrives
in the **by serial** bucket without anybody walking out to the ramp. Read it against the data
plate once: the federal file is a copy of somebody else's paperwork, and plenty of rows in it
carry no serial number at all.

Expect to type some of them. The lookup covers **US registrations only**, so a club with a
European sailplane gets no matches and enters the serial itself. A tail the lookup cannot find is
an ordinary answer rather than an error: nothing is broken, you type the fields instead. And any
aircraft added before the lookup began filling this field still has an empty one.

Choose any aeroplane in that list to open its aircraft form without leaving the page. It opens
with the cursor already in **Serial number**, so there is nothing to scroll for. Type it, save,
and the list corrects itself. The number is on the data plate, and it is on the aircraft's
registration. A fleet of more than six gets a search box above the list, so you can find one tail
without reading all of them.

<Callout type="note">
A serial number is not required to turn anything on. Matching by model still works; it is just
noisier. We would rather show you a longer list and say why than block the feature behind an
afternoon of data entry.
</Callout>

## Who can change this

Owners and admins. Not an office manager with the settings grant, and not a technician.

Turning the catalogue on changes what every technician at the school is asked to review, which
makes it a decision about how the school runs rather than a preference.

## Changing your mind

Switching modes never deletes anything. Compliance records are permanent regardless, so a school
that turns the catalogue off keeps every record it has already signed, and a school that switches
to *Somewhere else* keeps its history and simply stops being offered new candidates.
